## Overview

ROI LevelUp is a multi-company SaaS platform for running day-to-day business operations from a
single system: customers, suppliers, CRM pipelines, appointments, internal communication,
subscription billing, and invoicing. It ships publicly as a unified suite of content, CRM, outreach,
scheduling, and analytics tools. Every company on the platform gets its own isolated data, roles,
and navigation, which made tenancy and access control the central design problem rather than an
afterthought.

**Stack:** Laravel · CodeIgniter · MySQL · Stripe · Firebase

## What I built

### Backend architecture and multi-tenancy

I developed the backend architecture for company-specific roles, permissions, and menus, so that
what a user can see and do is derived from their company context rather than a single global role
table. On top of that sit the business domains — CRM pipelines, deals, offers, appointments,
invoices, and the workflows that connect them.

### Stripe subscription lifecycle

I implemented the full subscription lifecycle rather than just a checkout flow:

- Trial periods and their expiry behaviour
- Failed payment handling and retry states
- Cancellations, including what a company retains after cancelling
- Plan changes with upgrade proration
- Webhook handling to keep platform state in sync with Stripe as the source of truth
- Company-level access control driven by subscription status

The webhook layer is the part that matters most in production — billing state changes originate at
Stripe, and the platform has to react to them correctly even when the user is not in the browser.

### Real-time communication and video

I built Firebase-based 1-to-1 and group chat, then integrated appointment-based video
communication on top of the existing scheduling system. That covered recordings, transcripts,
notifications, and file attachments, so a booked appointment produces a durable record rather than
an ephemeral call.

## Outcome

ROI LevelUp runs in production as a multi-company platform. The subscription and access-control layer is
the piece I return to most often — it sits between the billing provider and every feature gate in
the product, so correctness there determines whether customers can use what they paid for.
